\section{Introduction}
The goal of this assignment is to design, implement and experimentally evaluate
an evolutionary algorithm for a combinatorial optimisation problem. We chose the
Travelling Salesman Problem, whose discrete, permutation-based search space and
relevance to routing and logistics make it a natural and challenging testbed for
evolutionary computation. This report presents our problem formulation, the
evolutionary approach and operators we developed, and an experimental analysis
of its solution quality and convergence behaviour.

% ===============================================================
\section{The Travelling Salesman Problem}

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation}

The Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) is a classical NP-hard combinatorial
optimisation problem: given a set of $N$ cities and the distances between them,
the task is to find the shortest closed tour that visits every city exactly
once.
Following the formulation of Potvin~\cite{potvin1996}, we model the problem
on a complete weighted graph $G=(V,E)$, where $V=\{1,\dots,N\}$ is the set of
cities and $d_{ij}$ is the distance between cities $i$ and $j$. The decision
variables are $x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$, where $x_{ij}=1$ if and only if the tour
travels directly from city $i$ to city $j$. The objective is to minimise the
total travelled distance,
\begin{equation}
  \text{Minimise} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} d_{ij}\,x_{ij},
  \label{eq:tsp_objective}
\end{equation}
subject to the degree constraints
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{N} x_{ij}=1, \quad i=1,\dots,N,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_{ij}=1, \quad j=1,\dots,N,
  \label{eq:tsp_degree}
\end{equation}
which require every city to have exactly one incoming and one outgoing edge,
and the subtour-elimination constraints
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i,j\in S} x_{ij} \;\le\; |S|-1,
  \qquad \forall\, S\subset V,\; 2\le |S|\le N-1,
  \label{eq:tsp_subtour}
\end{equation}
which forbid partial cycles over any proper subset $S$ of the cities.

Because an evolutionary algorithm operates on orderings rather than on binary
edge variables, it is convenient to state the equivalent tour-as-permutation
formulation. A candidate solution is a permutation
$\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_N)$ of the cities, and its cost is the length of the
cycle that visits them in that order,
\begin{equation}
  C(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} d_{\pi_k,\pi_{k+1}} \;+\; d_{\pi_N,\pi_1}.
  \label{eq:tsp_permutation}
\end{equation}
The evolutionary algorithm therefore seeks a permutation minimising
Equation~\eqref{eq:tsp_permutation}.

% ===============================================================
\subsection{Related Evolutionary Approaches}
\label{sec:related}

Liu~\cite{liu2014} builds a genetic algorithm around a crossover operator
called \emph{Edge Swapping} (ES), which keeps the edges shared by the two
parent tours and swaps in the cycles where they disagree; seeding the
population with greedy tours refined by 2-opt, it reaches best-known solutions
on large benchmarks. Liao et al.~\cite{liao2012} propose GPSO, a genetic-based
particle swarm optimisation in which each particle is a real-valued vector
decoded into a tour by ranking its components, initialised geometrically by
sorting cities by their angle around the geographical centre, which produces a
non-crossing route. For the path representation, where a chromosome is the
visiting order and classical one-point or uniform crossovers cannot be used as
they duplicate cities, Hussain et al.~\cite{hussain2017} compare partially
mapped (PMX), order (OX) and cycle crossover, where a segment of one parent is
copied into the child and the remaining cities are filled in their order of
appearance in the other parent; no operator dominates on TSPLIB.

% ===============================================================
\subsection{Motivation of the Developed Approach}

The evolutionary approach we develop follows the genetic algorithm presented in
Section~9.2 of Mitchell~\cite{mitchell1997}, adapted to the permutation
representation of the TSP; its pseudo-code is given in
Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}. Within this template, the design choices left open
concern the initialization of the population and the crossover operator, which
we align with the literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}. We
test four initializations: random, greedy nearest-neighbour tours refined by
2-opt~\cite{liu2014}, geometric non-crossing tours~\cite{liao2012}, and their
combination. For recombination we adopt the order
crossover (OX)~\cite{hussain2017}. The remaining operators are left
unchanged, so that the effect of the two adapted components can be isolated in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental}.

\section{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}
Each individual is represented as a permutation of all cities, where the order
of the cities defines the tour and the final city is connected back to the first.
The initial population consists of $p$ tours generated by the selected
initialization method.
The cost of an individual is given by Equation~\eqref{eq:tsp_permutation}.
Since the TSP is a minimisation problem, this cost is converted into a
maximisation-based fitness function defined as
\begin{equation}
    F(\pi) = \frac{1}{C(\pi)}.
    \label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
Consequently, shorter tours receive higher fitness values and are favoured
during selection. In the implementation,
tour costs are used directly where possible, since minimizing $C(\pi)$ is
equivalent to maximizing $F(\pi)$.

In each generation, approximately a fraction $(1-r)$ of the population is
retained for the next generation: each survivor is chosen independently from
the current population by tournament selection of size $k$, and is copied to
the new population before mutation. The remaining fraction is filled with
offspring generated from selected parent pairs.

Parent selection for crossover is performed using tournament selection.
For each parent, $k$ individuals are sampled randomly from the current
population, and the individual with the lowest tour cost is selected.
The process is repeated to obtain the second parent. Tournament selection
provides selection pressure towards shorter tours while still allowing
different individuals to participate in reproduction.

Order Crossover (OX) is used to generate offspring because it preserves valid permutations. A segment from one parent is copied to the offspring,
while the remaining cities are inserted according to their order in the second parent, skipping already used cities. Two offspring are generated for each parent pair
by swapping the parent roles.

After crossover, mutation is applied to a fraction $m$ of the new population. 
The individuals to be mutated are selected uniformly at random.
Depending on the experiment, either swap mutation or inversion mutation is
used. Swap mutation exchanges two randomly selected positions in a tour,
whereas inversion mutation reverses the order of the cities within a randomly
selected segment. Both mutation operators preserve a valid permutation.

Once mutation has been applied, the new population replaces the previous one
and all tour costs are evaluated again. The best tour found so far is stored
throughout the run. This means that even if a previously good individual is
later modified by mutation or disappears from the population, the best solution
encountered during the search is not lost.

The process is repeated for $G$ generations. After termination, the best tour
encountered during the complete run is returned.


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Genetic Algorithm for the Travelling Salesman Problem}
\begin{algorithmic}
\Require cities $D$, population size $p$, replacement rate $r$, mutation rate $m$, number of generations $G$, tournament size $k$
\Ensure best tour found

\State $P \gets$ generate $p$ initial tours
\State evaluate the cost of each individual in $P$
\State $best \gets$ individual in $P$ with minimum cost

\For{$g = 1$ to $G$}
    \State create new generation $P_s \gets \emptyset$

    \Statex
    \State \textbf{Selection:}
    \State $q \gets \operatorname{round}(rp/2)$
    \State $s \gets p - 2q$
    \For{$i = 1$ to $s$}
        \State select an individual from $P$ using tournament selection of size $k$
        \State add it to $P_s$
    \EndFor

    \Statex
    \State \textbf{Crossover:}
    \For{$i = 1$ to $q$}
        \State select $h_1$ from $P$ using tournament selection of size $k$
        \State select $h_2$ from $P$ using tournament selection of size $k$
        \State $o_1 \gets \textsc{OrderCrossover}(h_1,h_2)$
        \State $o_2 \gets \textsc{OrderCrossover}(h_2,h_1)$
        \State add $o_1$ and $o_2$ to $P_s$
    \EndFor

     \Statex
    \State \textbf{Mutation:}
    \State uniformly select $mp$ individuals from $P_s$
    \For{each selected individual $\pi$}
        \State apply the selected mutation operator to $\pi$
    \EndFor

    \Statex
    \State \textbf{Update:}
    \State $P \gets P_s$

    \Statex
    \State \textbf{Evaluation:}
    \State evaluate the cost of each individual in $P$

    \If{the best individual in $P$ has lower cost than $best$}
        \State update $best$
    \EndIf
\EndFor

\State \Return $best$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental part}
\label{sec:experimental}

\subsection{Hardware and Software}
All experiments were run on two MacBook Pro laptops with Apple Silicon
(M-series) processors. The algorithm is implemented in Python~3.12 using only
the standard library; \texttt{matplotlib} is used solely to produce the
figures. Every trial runs as a single process. Random numbers are generated
from explicitly assigned seeds, making the experiments reproducible with the
same code and configuration.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
Each configuration is repeated for 30 runs. The study proceeds in three
stages. First, random, nearest-neighbour tours improved by 2-opt, angle-based
tours, and their combination are compared on burma14, berlin52, kroA100, and
kroA200. Second, population size $p \in \{50,100,120,150\}$, number of
generations $G \in \{100,300,500,800\}$, mutation rate
$m \in \{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4\}$, and replacement rate
$r \in \{0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$ are varied one at a time on the same four
instances and for all four initialization methods. The remaining parameters
are held at $p=100$, $G=500$, $m=0.1$, and $r=0.7$, and inversion mutation is
used. Third, the problem-size experiment uses random initialization and these
default parameter values on all six instances.

\subsection{Datasets}
The evaluation uses six symmetric TSPLIB instances~\cite{reinelt1991} of
increasing size: burma14 (14 cities), berlin52 (52), kroA100 (100), kroA200
(200), pcb442 (442) and pr1002 (1002). They are committed to the repository
and loaded with their original TSPLIB edge weights (GEO for burma14, EUC\_2D
for the others). Performance is measured as the optimality gap
$(C_{\mathrm{best}}-C_{\mathrm{opt}})/C_{\mathrm{opt}}\cdot 100$, using the
published optima 3323, 7542, 21282, 29368, 50778 and 259045. The main study
uses the four first datasets while the scaling experiment covers all six instances.
